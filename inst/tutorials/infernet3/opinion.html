This tutorial

The “Diffusion and Contagion” tutorial follows states that nodes adopt or not, such as an infection or an innovation. This tutorial follows opinions that are numbers, such as the probability that someone puts on a statement being true, or their position on a scale from left to right. Here a node does not adopt an opinion from one neighbour: it moves its opinion towards the opinions of the nodes it listens to.

Learning models

The simplest learning model is the DeGroot model. At each step, every node takes the average of the opinions of the nodes it listens to. A tie from one node to another means that the first node listens to the second. A node divides its attention among the nodes it listens to, in proportion to the tie weights (their absolute values, for a signed network). to_influence() shows these shares as a matrix. Under DeGroot, a node does not count its own opinion, unless the network has a tie from the node to itself. All nodes update at once at each step, and the ties do not change, whatever the opinions do. A question often asked of these models is whether, from different first opinions, the opinions converge through (network) interaction. A network that is strongly connected and aperiodic converges to a consensus of (any) opinions entered.

Expectations of convergence and consensus

Let’s try this out on the ison_networkers dataset of communication among network academics. Perhaps we wish to get them to change their local networks/behaviour, but they have different beliefs about whether this will make a difference. To see whether this network will converge to consensus, check whether the network is connected and aperiodic :

  • is_connected() marks whether every node can reach every other node. On a directed network, it follows the direction of the ties by default (strong connectivity); add connectivity = "weak" to ignore their direction.
  • is_aperiodic() marks whether network is aperiodic, meaning there is no integer k > 1 that divides the length of every cycle of the graph.
# By default is_connected() will check whether a directed network
# is strongly connected.
is_connected(ison_networkers)
is_aperiodic(ison_networkers)

Playing the DeGroot learning model

Now let’s see whether you are right. We want to see whether some random distribution of beliefs converges to a consensus in this network (ison_networkers). Let’s play the DeGroot learning model on this network with a vector of random beliefs (the same length as the nodes in the network) drawn from the binomial distribution with probability 0.25. Create the distribution of beliefs and graph the network to show where they have been distributed. Then play the learning model with play_opinion() and these beliefs, and plot the result.

beliefs <- rbinom(net_nodes(____), 1, prob = 0.25)

Each line in this plot represents the belief trajectory of a single node at each step. About a quarter of the nodes begin believing, and the other three quarters do not. Then we can see how responsive these nodes are to the random distribution of beliefs across the network. Some revise their beliefs more significantly than others. To see the opinion of each node at each step as a table, use as_opinion(). play_opinion() stops when no opinion moves by more than tolerance, so the last step is very near to the end, but not exactly at it.

How fast is a consensus?

Whether a network reaches a consensus is one question; how fast it reaches it is another. net_by_convergence() from {netrics} measures the share of the distance to the opinions at the end that is closed at each step, in the long run. A value of 1 settles in one step, and a value of 0 never settles. It needs only the network, not a played model. Compare the networkers with a complete network of the same size (32 nodes), and with two rings: one of 31 nodes and one of 32 nodes.

net_by_convergence(ison_networkers)
net_by_convergence(create_filled(32))
net_by_convergence(create_ring(31))
net_by_convergence(create_ring(32))

Stubbornness

In the DeGroot model, a strongly connected, aperiodic network always ends in a consensus. But many disagreements last. One reason is that people hold on to their first opinion. In the Friedkin–Johnsen model, each node has a stubbornness from 0 to 1: how much it keeps its first opinion, compared with what the network says. A stubbornness of 0 is DeGroot’s model, and a stubbornness of 1 is a node that never moves. Note that a stubborn node is pulled back to its first opinion, not to the opinion it held at the last step. That first opinion never changes.

A path of three

Let us make the smallest example: a path A–B–C, where A starts at 0, B at 0.5, and C at 1. create_explicit() makes the path from a formula. Play the opinions with no stubbornness, and then with a stubbornness of 0.5 for every node. Use as_opinion() to see the opinions at each step.

path <- create_explicit(A-B-C)
graphr(path)
as_opinion(play_opinion(path, c(0, 0.5, 1)))
as_opinion(play_opinion(path, c(0, 0.5, 1), stubbornness = ____))
path <- create_explicit(A-B-C)
as_opinion(play_opinion(path, c(0, 0.5, 1)))
as_opinion(play_opinion(path, c(0, 0.5, 1), stubbornness = 0.5))

Note that with stubbornness the opinions still settle, but they settle apart. Partly stubborn nodes are enough to keep a disagreement alive.

Bounded confidence

Another reason that disagreements last is that people listen only to those whose opinions are near enough to their own. (If you have ever made excuses to leave a dinner party early, you know this model: some people are just too different.) These are models of bounded confidence . confidence says how far apart two opinions can be for one node still to listen to the other. Two rules use it:

  • rule = "hegselmann_krause": all nodes at once take the average of the opinions of only those nodes they listen to whose opinions are within confidence of their own. Unlike DeGroot, each node always counts its own opinion here. This rule is deterministic.
  • rule = "deffuant": at each step, one node is drawn at random, and it draws one of the nodes it listens to. If their opinions are within confidence, each moves halfway towards the other. This rule is random, and it changes only one pair at each step, so it needs many more steps.

Clusters of opinion

Draw the first opinions of the networkers at random, from 0 to 1. Play the Hegselmann–Krause rule with a confidence of 0.1, and plot it.

Then see how the clusters change with the confidence. sweep_opinion() plays the rule for each setting that you give it in vary, here for each confidence from 0.05 to 0.5, and counts the clusters of opinion at the end of each. Give it 100 steps, so that the opinions have time to settle.

set.seed(1)
first <- runif(net_nodes(ison_networkers))
plot(play_opinion(ison_networkers, first, rule = "____", confidence = ____))
hk <- sweep_opinion(ison_networkers, first, rule = "____", steps = 100,
                    vary = list(confidence = seq(____, ____, 0.05)))
set.seed(1)
first <- runif(net_nodes(ison_networkers))
plot(play_opinion(ison_networkers, first, rule = "hegselmann_krause",
                  confidence = 0.1))
hk <- sweep_opinion(ison_networkers, first, rule = "hegselmann_krause",
                    steps = 100, vary = list(confidence = seq(0.05, 0.5, 0.05)))
hk

Random encounters

Think of the Deffuant rule as a series of coffee breaks: two colleagues meet, and if they are not too far apart, they meet in the middle. Now play the Deffuant rule on the same first opinions, with a confidence of 0.1 and 2000 steps. This rule is random, so each time it is played, its result can differ. So sweep it 10 times for each confidence from 0.1 to 0.5, and set a seed in control so that you can repeat the result.

set.seed(1)
first <- runif(net_nodes(ison_networkers))
plot(play_opinion(ison_networkers, first, rule = "deffuant",
                  confidence = 0.1, steps = 2000))
deffuant <- sweep_opinion(ison_networkers, first, rule = "deffuant",
                          steps = 2000, times = 10, control = list(seed = 2),
                          vary = list(confidence = seq(0.1, 0.5, 0.1)))
deffuant
plot(deffuant)

Diverse groups

Bounded confidence also matters for how we make groups, whether committees, seminar groups, or juries. node_in_roulette() from {netrics} puts the nodes into groups that are as diverse as possible on an attribute, such as their first opinions. Let us compare such maximally diverse groups with groups of like minds, where each group holds the nodes whose first opinions are nearest to one another. In each group, every member listens to every other member, which create_components() makes from a membership.

set.seed(1)
first <- runif(net_nodes(ison_networkers))
diverse <- node_in_roulette(ison_networkers, groups = 4,
                            attribute = first, balance = 1)
alike <- ceiling(rank(first) / 8)
diverse_groups <- create_components(32, membership = diverse)
alike_groups <- create_components(32, membership = alike)
# Play the DeGroot model in each kind of group, and average the end by group
ends <- unlist(tail(as_opinion(play_opinion(diverse_groups, first)), 1))
tapply(ends, diverse, mean)
set.seed(1)
first <- runif(net_nodes(ison_networkers))
diverse <- node_in_roulette(ison_networkers, groups = 4,
                            attribute = first, balance = 1)
alike <- ceiling(rank(first) / 8)
diverse_groups <- create_components(32, membership = diverse)
alike_groups <- create_components(32, membership = alike)
# DeGroot: the opinion that each group ends with
ends <- unlist(tail(as_opinion(play_opinion(diverse_groups, first)), 1))
tapply(ends, diverse, mean)
ends <- unlist(tail(as_opinion(play_opinion(alike_groups, first)), 1))
tapply(ends, alike, mean)
# Bounded confidence: the number of clusters at the end
sweep_opinion(diverse_groups, first, rule = "hegselmann_krause",
              vary = list(confidence = c(0.15, 0.3)))
sweep_opinion(alike_groups, first, rule = "hegselmann_krause",
              vary = list(confidence = c(0.15, 0.3)))

Diversity spreads the information that each group holds, but it helps only where members listen to opinions far from their own.

Repulsion

So far, every tie pulls a node towards the opinions of the nodes it listens to. But we may also move away from the opinions of those we dislike: if they are for it, I must be against it. In a signed network, a negative tie can stand for such repulsion: the node moves towards the opposite of the other node’s opinion. Opposite opinions are then opposite signs, so the opinions are on a scale centred on 0, from -1 to 1.

A signed network is structurally balanced where its nodes split into two camps, with positive ties inside each camp and negative ties between them. Where it is balanced, repulsion splits the opinions into two camps of opposite signs. Where it is not, the opinions are pulled towards 0.

The road to war

irps_wwi holds the alliances (positive) and enmities (negative) of six European powers from 1872 to 1918. to_time() gives the network in one year. Play the same first opinions on the network in 1890 and in 1914, and check the balance of each with net_by_balance().

first <- c(0.5, 0.2, 0.1, -0.1, -0.3, 0.4)

Who has influence?

Where the opinions reach a consensus, the consensus is a weighted mean of the first opinions. The weight of each node is its influence: node_by_influence() from {netrics} measures it. Without stubbornness, it is the left eigenvector of the influence matrix from to_influence(), where each row says whom a node listens to. This is also called the social power of a node. node_by_influence() and node_by_opinion() compute the exact end, not a played one, so they need no steps. If the network is periodic, the opinions swing for ever, and these functions give the mean of the swing.

Influence and eigenvector centrality

It is often said that the most influential nodes in a network are those with the highest eigenvector centrality. Compare node_by_influence() with node_by_eigenvector() for the networkers.

node_by_influence(ison_networkers)
node_by_eigenvector(ison_networkers)
cor(node_by_influence(ison_networkers), node_by_eigenvector(ison_networkers))
ison_networkers |>
  mutate_nodes(influence = node_by_influence(ison_networkers)) |>
  graphr(node_size = "influence")

Wisdom of crowds

Imagine that each networker makes an estimate of some true value, for example the number of papers in a journal, with some error. The mean of all their estimates is often close to the truth, since the errors cancel out: the wisdom of crowds . Francis Galton found that the crowd at a country fair guessed the weight of an ox to within one percent, better than most of the experts there. But what if they talk first, and then agree on one consensus? The consensus is the mean of their first estimates, weighted by the influence of each node. Such a crowd stays wise only where no node has too much influence.

Does talking make the crowd wiser?

Let the true value be 50, and let each estimate miss it by an error with a standard deviation of 10. The normalized = FALSE influence of each node is its weight in the consensus, so sum(w * errors) is the error of the consensus. Draw the errors 1000 times for the networkers, a star, and a complete network, and compare the spread of the error of the consensus.

One catch: a star is periodic, so its opinions would swing between the centre and the leaves for ever (remember the rings above?). So let every node also listen a little to itself, with to_looped(). That is realistic anyway: few of us drop our own estimate entirely.

set.seed(1)
estimates <- 50 + rnorm(32, 0, 10)
consensus <- play_opinion(ison_networkers, estimates)
tail(as_opinion(consensus), 1)
mean(estimates)
w <- node_by_influence(to_looped(ison_networkers), normalized = FALSE)
max(w)
sd(replicate(1000, sum(w * rnorm(32, 0, 10))))
for(net in list(ison_networkers, create_star(32), create_filled(32))){
  w <- node_by_influence(to_looped(net), normalized = FALSE)
  print(c(largest_influence = max(w),
          error_of_consensus = sd(replicate(1000, sum(w * rnorm(32, 0, 10))))))
}

When does talking help?

In the DeGroot model, talking cannot make the crowd wiser on average: the consensus weights the estimates unequally, and the plain mean, with equal weights, has the smallest expected error. But in experiments, Becker, Brackbill and Centola (2017) found that talking made crowds wiser, if their network was egalitarian: if no node had much more influence than the others. One reason is that people whose first estimate is near the truth tend to revise it less. So the accurate people are more stubborn.

Let the stubbornness of each node be 1 minus its error, as a share of the largest error. Then the most accurate node is a zealot, and the least accurate is not stubborn at all. node_by_opinion() gives the opinion of each node at the end, from the nodal attributes “opinions” and “stubbornness”. Compare the error of the mean before and after the nodes talk, 200 times, in a random network, a star, and the networkers, each with to_looped() as before.

set.seed(1)
estimates <- 50 + rnorm(32, 0, 10)
error <- abs(estimates - 50)
crowd <- to_looped(generate_random(32, 0.2)) |>
  mutate_nodes(opinions = estimates, stubbornness = 1 - error / max(error))
ends <- node_by_opinion(crowd)
c(before = abs(mean(estimates) - 50), after = abs(mean(ends) - 50))
set.seed(1)
nets <- list(random = generate_random(32, 0.2), star = create_star(32),
             networkers = ison_networkers)
for(name in names(nets)){
  wiser <- replicate(200, {
    estimates <- 50 + rnorm(32, 0, 10)
    error <- abs(estimates - 50)
    crowd <- to_looped(nets[[name]]) |>
      mutate_nodes(opinions = estimates, stubbornness = 1 - error / max(error))
    ends <- node_by_opinion(crowd)
    abs(mean(ends) - 50) < abs(mean(estimates) - 50)
  })
  print(c(network = name, wiser = mean(wiser)))
}

Misinformation

What happens when some nodes spread a false opinion and never change it? (Every group chat seems to have one.) Such a fully stubborn node is sometimes called a zealot . In the DeGroot model, one zealot in a strongly connected network pulls every other node to its opinion in the end. Where the others are also a little stubborn, the pull is partial, and it depends on where the zealot sits.

Spreading a false claim and believing it are two different processes. Passing a story on is a diffusion: each node has heard the story or not, as in the “Diffusion and Contagion” tutorial. Believing it is learning: how much a node believes the story moves towards what the nodes it listens to believe. A story can reach everyone and convince no one, or reach only a few and still shift what the whole network believes. This section is about belief.

Where does misinformation do most harm?

Let the truth be 0, and let the networkers start near it. Then let one node be a zealot at 1, and all others hold on to their first opinion with a stubbornness of 0.3. Compare a zealot with the highest influence and one with the lowest, by the mean opinion at the end.

set.seed(2)
first <- rnorm(32, 0, 0.1)
influence <- node_by_influence(ison_networkers)
zealot <- which.max(influence)
opinions <- first
opinions[zealot] <- 1
stubborn <- rep(0.3, 32)
stubborn[zealot] <- ____
ends <- tail(as_opinion(play_opinion(ison_networkers, opinions,
                                     stubbornness = stubborn, steps = 500)), 1)
mean(unlist(ends))
set.seed(2)
first <- rnorm(32, 0, 0.1)
influence <- node_by_influence(ison_networkers)
for(zealot in c(which.max(influence), which.min(influence))){
  opinions <- first
  opinions[zealot] <- 1
  stubborn <- rep(0.3, 32)
  stubborn[zealot] <- 1
  ends <- tail(as_opinion(play_opinion(ison_networkers, opinions,
                                       stubbornness = stubborn, steps = 500)), 1)
  print(c(zealot = node_labels(ison_networkers)[zealot],
          mean_opinion = round(mean(unlist(ends)), 3)))
}

What could stop a zealot? Try a second zealot at the truth, 0, as a fact-checker, and move it to different places in the network.

Moving the window

A zealot whose opinion is far from everyone else’s has no pull where people listen only to opinions near their own. Joseph Overton described a “window” of the policies that the public will accept at a given time. A policy outside the window is unthinkable, whoever argues for it. But the window can move. In a model of bounded confidence, each node’s confidence is such a window, centred on its own opinion.

Let the networkers start between 0 and 0.4, and let the node with the most influence be a zealot. Play the Hegselmann–Krause rule with a confidence of 0.2. First, let the zealot jump straight to 1. Then let the zealot move in small steps of 0.1, from 0.3 to 1, and let each step start from the opinions at the end of the step before.

set.seed(1)
first <- runif(32, 0, 0.4)
zealot <- which.max(node_by_influence(ison_networkers))
stubborn <- ifelse(seq_len(32) == zealot, 1, 0)
# One jump
jump <- first
jump[zealot] <- 1
ends <- tail(as_opinion(play_opinion(ison_networkers, jump, rule = "hegselmann_krause",
                                     confidence = ____, stubbornness = stubborn,
                                     steps = 100)), 1)
mean(unlist(ends)[-zealot])

# Small steps
opinions <- first
for(step in seq(0.3, 1, ____)){
  opinions[zealot] <- step
  opinions <- unlist(tail(as_opinion(play_opinion(ison_networkers, ____,
                                                  rule = "hegselmann_krause",
                                                  confidence = 0.2,
                                                  stubbornness = stubborn,
                                                  steps = 100)), 1))
  print(c(zealot = step, others = round(mean(opinions[-zealot]), 2)))
}

So an opinion that no one will hear today can become acceptable tomorrow, if the opinions in between are heard first. Try a wider or a narrower confidence, or larger steps. How large can a step be before the window stops following?

Equilibria and disequilibria

Each model in this tutorial settles in its own way, or does not settle at all:

Outcome What gives it Where we saw it
Consensus A strongly connected, aperiodic network, with no stubbornness DeGroot on ison_networkers
Lasting disagreement Stubbornness, which pulls each node back to its first opinion The path A–B–C
Fragmentation Bounded confidence, which cuts the ties between distant opinions Hegselmann–Krause and Deffuant
Polarisation Repulsion on a balanced signed network irps_wwi in 1914
Capture One zealot in a network with no other stubborn node Misinformation
No equilibrium A periodic network, where opinions can swing for ever A ring with an even number of nodes

The last is a disequilibrium . On a ring of four nodes, each node averages its two neighbours. Start with opinions of 1, 0, 1, and 0, and see what happens at each step. Then try a ring of five, with 1, 0, 1, 0, and 0.

as_opinion(play_opinion(create_ring(4), c(1, 0, 1, 0), steps = 6))
is_aperiodic(create_ring(4))
as_opinion(play_opinion(create_ring(5), c(1, 0, 1, 0, 0), steps = 50))
is_aperiodic(create_ring(5))

A real group may still settle on such a network, because people also listen to themselves. A self-weight, a self-loop in the network, breaks the swing. Try play_opinion(to_looped(create_ring(4)), c(1, 0, 1, 0)), and see to_influence() for how the self-weight is set.

Glossary

Where opinions end up depends on whom each node listens to, and how much. In the DeGroot model, a network that is strongly connected and aperiodic always reaches a consensus, and how fast it gets there depends on its structure. Stubbornness keeps a disagreement alive, because each node is pulled back towards its first opinion. Bounded confidence breaks a group into clusters of opinion, since nodes stop listening to opinions too far from their own, so diverse groups learn from one another only where their members listen widely. Repulsion on a balanced signed network polarises opinions into two camps. The nodes with the most influence on the end are close to, but not the same as, those with the highest eigenvector centrality. Talking can make a crowd wiser, but only where no node has too much influence, and a zealot does most harm where it has most influence. A zealot too far from the others is not heard, but one that moves in small steps can move the whole window of acceptable opinions. Finally, not every network settles: on a periodic network, opinions can swing for ever.

Here are some of the terms that we have covered in this module:

Aperiodic
An aperiodic network is a network where the greatest common divisor of the lengths of its cycles is one.
Balance
A signed network is structurally balanced where its nodes split into two camps, with positive ties inside each camp and negative ties between them.
Bounded Confidence
In a bounded confidence model, a node listens only to those whose opinions are within some distance of its own.
Connected
A connected network is one with a single (strong) component.
Periodic
A periodic network is one where the lengths of all its cycles share a divisor greater than one, so that a process on it can swing for ever.
Stubbornness
The stubbornness of a node is how much it holds on to its first opinion, from 0 for none to 1 for a node that never moves.
Wisdom Of Crowds
The wisdom of crowds is that the mean of many independent estimates is often closer to the truth than most of the estimates.
Zealot
A zealot is a fully stubborn node, with a stubbornness of 1, that never changes its opinion, however the others try to persuade it.
